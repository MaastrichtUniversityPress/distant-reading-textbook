# Body Image in Social Media

The following sample analysis is based on a dataset collected by **MA Digital Cultures student [Caroline Déharbe](https://nl.linkedin.com/in/caroline-d%C3%A9harbe-013669185/fr)** during the 2024/2025 academic year and reuses the academic literature and theoretical approaches from Caroline's original essay. It demonstrates how **Voyant Tools** can be used to analyze societal discussions around beauty standards, gender roles, and cultural expectations in **podcast reviews**.

### Why study body image in social media data?

Body image is a pressing topic in our digital age as social media and AI tools such as *beauty filters* influence self-worth, mental health, and societal norms. Social media platforms, podcasts, and video content also serve as spaces where these issues are controversially debated. Analyzing user-generated content — such as podcast reviews or YouTube comments — we can uncover how specific beauty standards are constructed, perpetuated, or resisted, what role gender and ethnicity play in the discussions, and how digital public spheres contribute to personal empowerment and collective activism. 

## Possible theoretical approaches

These analytical lenses can be linked to the three key theories that we discuss in the MA Digital Cultures course *Machines of Knowledge* at Maastricht University:

1. **Feminist Theory** examines women-led critiques of beauty standards, gendered communication patterns, and the impact of content creators’ (intersectional) identities on audience reactions.
2. **Postcolonial Theory** explores **cultural and racial dimensions** of body image discussions and can us help consider how marginalized voices engage with global perspectives.
3. **Public Sphere theory** is another possible approach and can draw your attention to how media such as podcasts create participatory spaces and contribute to (political) opinion-making. You can, for example, explore whether reviews and comments reflect a shift toward inclusive and democratic conversations, and if the users are empowered to take collective action offline.

# A Feminist Analysis of Themes in Anti-Diet Culture Podcast Reviews

## Introduction: the ubiquity of diet culture

Diet culture, a societal trend that promotes prescribed diets, weight loss, and rigid body ideals, has become a frequent topic in public discourse. Often framed as a women’s health issue (Jovanovski & Jaeger, 2022), its impact extends across ages and genders. In different cultural settings, exposure to diet culture online contributes to body image insecurities and disorders like anorexia and bulimia. Jacob & Panwar (2023) surveyed an Indian population and found considerable impact of social media usage on eating habits and appearance confidence, with certain gender and age differences in areas like snacking and convenience behaviors, social dependence, height dissatisfaction, or fitness evaluation. As digital platforms promote dieting advice by experts and non-expert influencers alike, podcasts such as *I Weigh with Jameela Jamil* and *Maintenance Phase* have become spaces for critique and anti-diet activism. These podcasts, hosted by experts, activists, or individuals affected by diet culture, use science and personal narratives to engage international audiences.

Unlike social media platforms (e.g., Instagram’s #womenswellness, as studied by Tiusanen, 2023), podcasts typically offer longer, structured content without immediate audience interaction. Apple Store reviews, however, allow listeners to reflect on the podcast as a whole. This essay explores in how far the podcast reviewers have called out systemic issues like gender inequality, racism, and capitalism as the core challenges of diet culture, and what they propose to counter them. For this approach, data feminism (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020) provides a suitable framework.

## Data selection and data collection

From the many podcasts opposing diet culture available on the Apple app store, I have selected ten English-language podcasts for this analysis. English-language shows have listeners from all over the world and receive more reviews than podcasts in other languages. Also, I have deliberately combined shows from different types of content creators: While some podcasts are hosted by celebrities, others are made by healthcare professionals, which will ideally also result in a broad range of opinions voiced in the comments.

**[Body Kindness](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/body-kindness/id123456789)** is hosted by a dietitian and physiologist, who focus on reframing health away from strict dieting. Similarly, **[In Bad Taste](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/in-bad-taste/id123456789)** is hosted by a nutritionist and surgeon, who critique health documentaries. **[Life After Diets](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/life-after-diets/id987654321)** features a health coach and psychotherapist addressing dieting’s shortcomings.
Other podcasts are made by non-experts and often have a stronger entertainment component. These include **[Fad Camp](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/fad-camp/id987654321)**, a comedic take on fad diets, **[Food, We Need To Talk](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/food-we-need-to-talk/id112233445)**, **[Maintenance Phase](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/maintenance-phase/id1564530722)**, and **[I Weigh with Jameela Jamil](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/i-weigh/id1498855031)**, a podcast hosted by actress and activist Jameela Jamil.
Last but not least, I have included three podcasts by self-identified victims of weight discrimination and societal pressure, namely **[Rebel Eaters Club](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/rebel-eaters-club/id112233445)**, **[We Can Do Hard Things](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/we-can-do-hard-things/id1564530722)**, and **[Weight For It](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/weight-for-it/id123456789)**, a "fat man’s" perspective on weight and diet.

The comments for these podcasts were scaped with the Python script provided in the MA Digital Cultures course and make up my final dataset of over 5,500 reviews. This data set is large enough for both quantitative insights (word frequencies) and qualitative close reading (contextualizing terms).

## Theoretical approach: Data feminism as a way to highlight social dimensions

Jovanovski and Jaeger (2024) identify two primary strategies for resisting diet culture: individual effort and social dimension. The first strategy emphasizes the need for individuals to dismantle internalized beliefs. The second strategy underscores the importance of collective action, as diet culture is perpetuated through social interactions and societal norms. According to Jovanovski and Jaeger (2022), the social dimension of health and body debates is closely linked with systemic oppressions through financial dependencies, racism, or patriarchy. To analyse what roles the social dimension plays in my collected podcast reviews, I will apply a data feminist approach. 
Data feminism (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020) emphasizes that we have to carefully examine power structures such as economic imbalances, challenge binaries, and elivate marginalized voices such as racial or gender minorities. This aligns with Jovanovski and Jaegers systemic oppressions and will help me aswer the research question.

## Methodology: Distant and close reading with Voyant

### The second structural challenge: racial steoreotyping

The theme of racism was more frequently and even more controversially addressed. Some listeners praised the podcasts for addressing racial diversity and fostering inclusive conversations. For instance, reviews highlighted how discussions on race helped them reflect on their own biases or societal expectations. However, other reviews accused the hosts or guests of harboring racist undertones. Among these, three reviews pointed to racism directed at non-white individuals, two mentioned perceived racism against white people (also described as "reseved racism"), and one remained ambiguous in its accusation. Additionally, three reviews referenced racism in contexts unrelated to the podcasts themselves, such as broader societal issues.

### Discussion: Podcasts as spaces of commercialization critique

The analysis revealed that capitalism and commericalization were the structural challenge most often mentioned across the different podcast. Gender and race were addressed to a lesser degree?? 

At the same time, the social dimension of resistance was evident in the reviews. Listeners described how the podcasts encouraged them to address systemic issues in their personal relationships, whether by setting boundaries or engaging in conversations about diet culture with friends and family.

The role of digital spaces like podcasts in fostering critical conversations about diet culture has also been confirmed in other research. Pope and Rose (2024), for example, demonstrate how public figures like Taylor Swift, through openness about personal struggles with body image and disordered eating, can normalize resistance to diet culture. Similarly, the hosts and guests of the podcasts analyzed in this study provided listeners with a sense of validation and community. Many reviewers explicitly stated that the podcasts made them feel "seen" and "heard". Jovanovski et al. (2024) even found that exposure to lived experiences can even influence the practices of healthcare professionals.

### Limitations

This essay is not without limitations. One significant constraint is the platform bias as the analysis was limited to Apple Store reviews, which may not be representative of the entire audience for these podcasts. Different platforms, such as Spotify or Google Podcasts, might attract distinct demographic groups, which could not be analyzed here.

Another limitation lies in the algorithmic blind spots of tools like Voyant. The contextual ambiguity of certain terms like "white" and "black" is one example. They may not always refer to race, and I have used the contexts tools to very their usage, but errors may still occur. Similarly, terms like "dominance" could apply to any of the systemic issues (patriarchy, racism, capitalism), leading to potential misclassifications. 

## Conclusion

This essay showed that many listeners of diet culture podcasts see them as important platforms for critical conversations and for creating a sense of collective resistance. In my dataset, resistance to the commericialisation of health was the most dominant theme brought up by reviewers. Future research could expand on these findings in several ways. Comparing reviews across multiple platforms, such as Spotify and Google Podcasts, and including content in several languages could offer a more comprehensive view of listener perspectives.
