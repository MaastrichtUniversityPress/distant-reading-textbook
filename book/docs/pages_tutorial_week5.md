### Task 5.1: Homework Discussion – Distant Reading in Practice

In this session, we will discuss experiences with **applying distant reading techniques** to your own research or case studies. Distant reading, as introduced by Franco Moretti, involves **analyzing large corpora of text to identify patterns, themes, and structures** that might not be visible through close reading alone.

We will explore how distant reading can be used to **study cultural phenomena, media narratives, and public discourse**. For example, how can you use tools like Voyant to **analyze trends in social media comments, podcast reviews, or news articles**? What insights can you gain from **word frequency analysis, co-occurrence networks, or sentiment analysis**?

This session will also address the **challenges and limitations** of distant reading, such as the need for **clean data, appropriate tools, and critical interpretation**. By the end of the discussion, you will have a clearer understanding of how to **integrate distant reading into your research workflow** and how to use it to support your arguments.

A good public sphere is seen as the elixir of modern democracy, as it provides a forum for exchanging views and formulating opinions (Habermas, 1962; Fraser, 1990). The public sphere is neither a private environment nor a sphere of bureaucratic or capitalist, instrumental rationality. Jürgen Habermas, in The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere 
 (1989 [1962]), describes and analyses the emergence and development of the public sphere. Habermas’ influential book is an inquiry into the social conditions for critical debates by citizens, based on the assumptions that arguments rather than hierarchies should determine their decisions. As such, it is argued that discursive ‘public will formation’ is one of the pillars of modern democracy. Habermas does not primarily stress what a public sphere ‘is’, but how certain social dynamics make a public sphere possible.
Media play an important role in structural transformations of the public sphere. The printing press, for example, was very influential for the emergence of the modern public sphere. Different media allow individuals to engage with content and their social environments in distinct ways. But how have digital media transformed the very structure of the public sphere, and how does technology intervene as a means to create new narratives and as a medium for public debate? Digital media and platforms, such as social networking sites can function as virtual spaces where people deliberate about different issues. Habermas described the public sphere not as ‘thing’, but as a matter of interaction. Such interaction is now largely digitally mediated. Some scholars argue that digital technologies may create new opportunities for civic engagement and participation. They have likewise examined how emerging ‘new public spheres’ may affect established public spheres − potentially in detrimental ways. Considering the broad range of new public spheres and social interaction taking place in these contexts, it is still under investigation if Habermas’ original concept is still applicable today. Notions such as private spheres, agonistic public spheres, and anti-public spheres have emerged as a response to questions of relevance of public spheres today.
Critical theorists have questioned blind spots in Habermas’ theory. According to Fraser (1990) and Papacharissi (2010), certain inequalities appear under-emphasized in his theory. They discuss inter alia how technology affects the politics, perceptions, and practices of gender with particular regard to public spheres. In doing so, these authors have suggested critical perspectives in response to Habermas’ critical theory. Finally, several scholars have argued that Habermas’ notion of the Public Sphere should be reformulated, if not abandoned (Webster 2013), to conform to the current, emerging, and more complex forms of public spheres.
