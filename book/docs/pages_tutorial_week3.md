### Task 3.1: Homework Discussion – Exploring Digital Data Sources I: Databases and Digital Scholarly Editions

In this session, we will discuss academic databases and digital scholarly editions as sources of data for research in media studies and digital humanities. Databases provide structured, searchable collections of information, while digital scholarly editions offer curated, annotated texts that can be analyzed for patterns, themes, and historical context.

We will explore how these sources differ from **traditional archives or print editions** and how they can be used to support computational analysis. For example, databases often include metadata (e.g., dates, authors, subjects) that can help you **filter and organize large datasets**, while digital editions may include annotations or contextual information that enriches your understanding of the text.

During the discussion, we will consider the **advantages and limitations** of using these sources. For instance, while databases and digital editions are often **clean and well-organized**, they may also be limited in scope or require additional processing to fit your research needs. We will also discuss how to **access and use these sources effectively** in your own projects.

By the end of this session, you will have a clearer understanding of how to **leverage databases and digital scholarly editions** in your research, and how they can complement other types of data, such as social media or user-generated content.


### **Task 3.2: Exploring Digital Data Sources II: Social Media**

Social media platforms are **rich sources of data** for studying public discourse, cultural trends, and audience engagement. In this session, we will explore how platforms like Twitter, YouTube, and Reddit generate vast amounts of user-generated content that can be analyzed to uncover patterns in communication, sentiment, and behavior.

We will discuss the **unique characteristics of social media data**, such as its **real-time nature, informality, and interactivity**. For example, social media comments and posts often reflect **spontaneous reactions** to events, making them valuable for studying public opinion or cultural phenomena. However, they can also be **noisy and unstructured**, requiring careful processing and analysis.

This session will introduce you to the **basics of collecting and analyzing social media data**, including ethical considerations and platform-specific challenges. We will also discuss how **playlists and curated collections** can help you organize and analyze larger sets of relevant content.

By the end of the session, you will have a better understanding of how to **use social media as a research source** and how to apply computational methods to analyze it effectively.
