### Task 4.2: Participatory Culture

Participatory culture refers to the ways in which audiences actively engage with, contribute to, and shape media and cultural content. The concept of participatory culture is important in digital media studies as present-day digital platforms enable users to create, share, and remix content (Jenkins, Ito & boyd, 2017). Jenkins et al. (2013) argue that the invention of social media has shifted the relationship between the creators and consumers of content. The public is no longer satisfied to be merely consumers of content but actively seeks to participate in the production of it. This is also visible in recent hashtag activism movements (Khazraee & Novak, 2018; Clark-Parsons, 2021) like #metoo, #FreeBritney, or #AshaAmini. However, scholars such as Christian Fuchs (2014) also consider the downsides of widespread user participation. The fact that a participatory web gives more people the possibility of spreading their ideas is not necessarily beneficial to democratic societies. Scholars such as Porlezza (2019), have also been noticing that due to phenomena such as trolls, incivility, and hate speech, participation fatigue has emerged. Similarly, mainstream social media platforms have gradually started being replaced by alternative, decentralised platforms where so-called "filter bubbles" can evolve even faster (Mannell and Smith 2022). 

**Group Activity: Mapping Participatory Platforms**

Work in groups of 3 or 4 to analyze two platforms each. You will create one mindmap per platform and share both on the course Canvas discussion board set up for this activity. We will compare all results in the plenary discussion afterwards.

**Groups:**
- Group 1: X and Instagram
- Group 2: TikTok and YouTube
- Group 3: Reddit and Discord
- Group 4: Mastodon and Bluesky
- Group 5: Twitch and one other streaming platform of your choice (for example, Kick or YouTube Live)

**What to do:**

For each platform, create one mindmap covering the following aspects:

1. Typical user groups: Who uses the platform (age groups, communities, roles)?
2. Typical content and interaction formats: What is shared, and how do users interact (posts, streams, comments, upvotes, direct messages)?
3. Problematic content: What harmful content can circulate there (deepfakes, disinformation, hate speech, harassment)?
4. Countermeasures: What does the platform itself do against such content (moderation, labelling, community guidelines, reporting tools)? Assess briefly whether these measures fit the platform's participatory logic.

You may use PowerPoint, Canva, the Teams whiteboard, Miro, or paper to create your boards. Photograph hand-drawn versions for sharing. You may use generative AI as a research aid, especially if you do not use a platform yourself. However, you must verify all information against reliable sources (platform documentation, terms of service, press coverage, or academic literature) and be prepared to name your sources.
